AI agents vs chatbots: what small business owners need to know

A chatbot answers. An agent acts. The difference matters because acting means access to your calendar, your records and sometimes your customers, so the question is not only what it can do but what it is allowed to do.

By Sulo Systems · Updated November 2026

Key takeaways

If you only have a minute.

  • A chatbot replies with text; an agent can use your tools to complete a task
  • Many businesses are well served by a chatbot, and some need an agent
  • The risk grows with what the software is allowed to do, so permissions matter more than the model
  • Start in a mode where the agent only suggests, and widen what it does as it earns trust
  • Always have a clear hand-off to a person

The short version

A chatbot is software that holds a conversation. Ask it the opening hours and it tells you. An agent is software that can carry out a task: it reads the situation, uses tools such as your calendar, email or database, takes the next step and asks a person when it is unsure.

Put simply, a chatbot can tell a customer which times are free. An agent can check the calendar, book the slot, update your records and send the confirmation.

When a chatbot is enough

If the job is answering common questions, such as hours, prices, what is included or how to get there, a chatbot built on your own information is often all you need. It is simpler, cheaper to run and easier to keep safe, because it does not touch anything. Many businesses should start here.

A chatbot also suits cases where a person needs to remain in the loop for every action. It gives the customer a quick answer and passes the rest to your team.

When you need an agent

Move to an agent when the value is in doing, not only answering: booking appointments, qualifying enquiries and scheduling the next step, creating records, chasing missing documents or keeping statuses up to date. These are multi-step jobs that currently eat someone’s day, and each step depends on what happened in the one before.

A good test is to ask whether a person would need to open another tool to finish the job after the chatbot has replied. If so, an agent is worth considering.

What changes when software can act

An agent that can act can also get things wrong in ways that matter. It can misread a situation, act on out-of-date information, or repeat a step. That is why the design is more important than the underlying model. The questions are what it can touch, what it can change, what needs a human to approve and how you will know what it did.

Sensible controls include giving it the least access that works, requiring approval for anything costly, public or hard to undo, limiting how much it can do in a given time, logging every action in a form you can read, and having a clear hand-off to a person when it is unsure.

How to start safely

Choose one job with a clear definition of success. Begin in suggest-only mode, where the agent proposes the action and your team approves it, so you can see how it behaves on real cases. Widen what it can do only as the record shows it handles each step reliably, and review a sample of what it does after launch.

If a vendor cannot explain these controls in plain language, treat that as an answer.

Questions to ask before you buy

What can it do, and what can it not do? Which of my systems will it connect to, and with what level of access? Which actions need a person’s approval? Can I read a log of everything it did? What happens when it is unsure? How do I switch off an ability? What does it cost to try on one job first?
